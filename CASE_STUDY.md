# Eight Days at Livadia: Roosevelt at Yalta, 1945

On 4 February 1945, Franklin Roosevelt prepared to meet Winston Churchill and Joseph Stalin at Livadia Palace, a former summer residence of the Russian tsars. Getting there had meant a voyage to Malta, a flight to Crimea and a long drive through the mountains. Barely two weeks into his fourth term as President, Roosevelt was ill and tired.1,2 Yet Germany's approaching defeat made an agreement on the peace increasingly urgent.3
For Roosevelt, that peace depended on continued Allied cooperation. He wanted Stalin's support for a new United Nations and Soviet help to defeat Japan, where the war continued.3 But cooperation also meant confronting their differences over Poland's government and Germany's future.3 Pressing Stalin on one question could complicate agreement on another, while concessions would have to be defended at home. With a short conference ahead, what should Roosevelt pursue first, and what was he prepared to give up?

## Two wars, two clocks — the War in February 1945

By February 1945, WWII had entered its final phase. Germany’s defeat appeared imminent, but the outcome of the peace remained uncertain. Without established post-war order and peace-keeping measures, the Allied Powers feared a repeat of the war that had devastated much of Europe.
In Eastern Europe, the USSR has gained the upper hand. The Red Army’s offensives in January have proven successful, allowing their troops to capture Warsaw.4 With the Red Army rapidly advancing to the Oder River, less than 100km from Berlin,5 it is clear that the USSR will play a decisive role in Germany’s defeat. In West Europe, the USA and UK have just won the Battle of the Bulge, turning Germany’s surprise offensive into a brutal counterattack.6 However, despite the victory, Allied troops suffered significant casualties in the conflict, and have yet to cross the Rhine.6
In the Pacific, the war against Japan rages on. American forces continue to advance but face fierce resistance from the Japanese. In particular, the rise in organised kamikaze (suicide) attacks7 has raised concerns that Japan would not surrender peacefully, even if Germany is defeated. With American casualties skyrocketing, Roosevelt is racing against the clock to secure victory. However, USA strategists feared that an invasion of Japan’s home islands would be costly, depleting the USA of resources and manpower.8

## Three men, three agendas

The alliance between the Big Three (USA, UK, USSR) was born out of a common enemy: Germany. United by war, the three countries now find themselves at risk of being divided by their own post-war ideals.
Roosevelt believes that maintaining cooperation between the Allies is crucial to prevent future conflict. At the Tehran Conference in 1943, he established a constructive working relationship with Stalin and came away determined that personal diplomacy could bridge their ideological differences. Most importantly, he obtained an informal agreement that the USSR would join the war against Japan after Germany’s defeat.9 Roosevelt is convinced that if he can continue building trust with Stalin, it may be possible to secure Soviet support for his interests.
However, it is evident that Stalin has his own set of priorities. The USSR had borne the brunt of the losses during the war,10 and Stalin demanded recognition and reparations for the USSR’s sacrifices. Furthermore, he places great emphasis on strengthening Soviet security through establishing friendly governments on Soviet borders.
Meanwhile, Churchill holds yet another perspective. The UK has been severely weakened by the war on multiple fronts, and no longer holds the influence in Europe it once did. Troubled by the rapid advance of Soviet troops in Eastern Europe, he fears that the Soviet sphere of influence may outgrow that of the UK.
With Churchill so wary of the USSR, Roosevelt knows that siding with his long-time ally may sour his relationship with Stalin. However, he cannot afford to lose the support of Churchill either.

## The weight on Roosevelt's shoulders

### On the home front
Roosevelt arrived at Yalta with fewer free hands than his position suggested. The first constraint was the US Senate, which would need to approve any treaty creating a world organisation by a two-thirds vote.11 Every American at Yalta remembered what had happened to Woodrow Wilson. In March 1920 the Senate rejected the Treaty of Versailles and, with it, American membership of the League of Nations.12 If the deal looked like a giveaway to Stalin, senators opposed to the new organisation could use it as their strongest argument for voting it down.
Roosevelt was also bound by his own words. In the Atlantic Charter of August 1941, he and Churchill had promised not to seek territorial gains and to respect the right of all peoples to choose their own government.13 Americans had been told that this was what the war was for. There were also votes at stake. At Tehran, Roosevelt had told Stalin privately that there were six to seven million Americans of Polish descent, and that he did not wish to lose their votes.14
### Thin leverage
Abroad, Roosevelt's leverage was weakest exactly where he needed it most. The Red Army, not American troops, held Poland.15 He could shape the wording of an agreement, but not the facts on the ground.
His strongest potential lever was money. On 3 January 1945, Soviet Foreign Minister Vyacheslav Molotov asked for a postwar American credit of six billion dollars to help rebuild the Soviet economy.16 But Lend-Lease, the programme through which America had supplied its allies during the war, was tied to the war effort. Ambassador Averell Harriman warned that this authority stopped when the fighting ended, and that any postwar credit would need new approval from Congress.16 Roosevelt could hint at aid. He could not promise it.
Finally, every issue drew on the same limited goodwill. Roosevelt needed Stalin for Japan, for Poland and for the new organisation, so pushing hard on one issue could make Stalin less willing on another. And time was short. Roosevelt’s health was declining fast,17 and a President this tired could not count on another summit to settle whatever Yalta left open.

## The road to Tokyo — War against Japan

On the Pacific front, American casualties piled up. Having joined WWII as a direct result of Japan’s attack on Pearl Harbour in 1941, the USA was eager to see the end of the Japan war.
### The price of invasion
Efforts to counter Japanese forces were already underway by early 1945. American troops were rapidly advancing across the Pacific. However, strategists warned that a land invasion could result in significant loss of lives, with estimates ranging from the hundreds of thousands to a million American casualties,18 and a 1944 report projecting the loss of half a million American troops.19
### An untested weapon
Meanwhile, the Manhattan Project, a secret programme designed to build an atomic bomb capable of wiping out Japan’s defences, had yet to deliver a functioning prototype. In December 1944, the Commanding General of the Manhattan Project estimated the first prototype would only be available around the 1st of August 1945.20 Furthermore, it was determined that the bombs would not require full-scale testing, meaning that the USA would be relying on untested and unfamiliar technology.20 With the effectiveness of the atomic bomb still largely uncertain, Roosevelt is wary to rely entirely on the Manhattan Project.
### Limited assistance
The UK was involved in the USA’s stand against Japan, with active British forces fighting in occupied territories like Burma,21 and navy assistance in the Pacific.22 However, the UK’s resources and military capacity had been stretched thin by 1945.
On the other hand, Stalin, who had previously already indicated his participation against Japan, had troops stationed in the Far East capable of attacking Japan’s Kwantung Army in Manchuria. However, a 1941 Soviet-Japanese Neutrality Pact23 meant that the USSR had no real obligation, as the two countries were not legally at war. In order for the USSR to assist the USA, Stalin would have to renounce the pact and put the USSR at risk of Japanese retaliation. This meant that significant concessions were likely necessary to persuade Stalin to abandon this neutrality.
How badly does Roosevelt require foreign aid in the war against Japan? What, or whose interests is he prepared to trade?


## Poland — a government behind Soviet lines

### Two governments, one country
When Roosevelt arrived at Yalta, the United States and Britain recognised Poland's government in exile in London, while the Soviet Union recognised the rival provisional government associated with Lublin.24 After Germany and the Soviet Union invaded Poland in September 1939, its government continued in exile, eventually based in London. Moscow had broken off relations with it in 1943.25 As Soviet troops advanced into Poland, Moscow sponsored a rival administration, the Lublin Committee. By the end of 1944 it declared itself the provisional government of Poland. Roosevelt had asked Stalin to delay recognising it until their meeting, but Stalin went ahead in January.26
### Three views of Poland
Roosevelt now had to find a settlement that could bring the Allies together. He wanted a government with broader Polish participation and free elections, while seeking a settlement Stalin would accept. He was also prepared to accept the Curzon Line as Poland's eastern border, leaving former Polish territory in Soviet hands, with German land offered as compensation. How much German territory Poland would receive remained unsettled.3 Accepting border changes, however, did not resolve who would govern Poland. An agreement that Americans saw as imposed by Moscow could undermine their confidence in cooperation through the proposed United Nations.24
Churchill shared the desire for an independent Poland. Britain had entered the war after Germany attacked it, and he needed a government he could recognise and defend before Parliament.27 He had accepted the Curzon Line but still sought Polish political independence.24,27 Stalin's goals centred around security. He wanted a friendly government on his border and supported Lublin rather than the return of the London government.28 Roosevelt wanted other Polish leaders included in the government, but Soviet military control gave Stalin a stronger position in deciding who could join and how much influence they would have.28,29
How much could Roosevelt demand before recognising a new government? Would bringing other Polish leaders into it give them real influence? And how far should he press Stalin while also seeking Soviet help against Japan and support for the United Nations?

## Building a better League

Roosevelt's greatest hope for peace was a new world organisation, the United Nations. He was determined that it would not repeat the failures of the League of Nations.
### Why the League failed
The League had three weaknesses. First, decisions in its Assembly and Council needed the agreement of every member present, so a single state could block action.30 Second, key powers, including the United States, stood outside it.12 When Italy invaded Abyssinia (Ethiopia) in 1935, the League's sanctions left out oil and were never fully applied, and Italy completed its conquest.31 Third, aggressors could simply leave. Japan walked out after being condemned over Manchuria,32 and Italy left in 1937.31 The League's strongest response, condemnation, produced exit rather than compliance.
The lesson Roosevelt drew was to bring the great powers in, keep them in and give the new body the means to act.
### Two open questions
In 1944, the United States, Britain, the Soviet Union and China met at Dumbarton Oaks in Washington to draft a charter. They agreed on a General Assembly of all members and a Security Council with the great powers as permanent members.33 Two questions were left open.
The first was voting in the Security Council. Roosevelt wanted a veto for the United States: no Senate would approve a body that could commit America against its will. But how far should a veto reach? Too weak, and the Soviet Union might not join, repeating the League's fatal flaw. Too strong, and the organisation could never act against a great power. Moscow had its own memory of the League: in December 1939, after the Soviet Union invaded Finland, the League’s members had voted to expel it.34 In December 1944 Roosevelt sent Stalin a compromise formula. Stalin rejected part of it, insisting that the permanent members must agree on all Council decisions and that none should be kept from voting.35
The second question was membership. The Soviet delegation had asked for all sixteen Soviet republics to join as separate members, each with its own vote in the Assembly.36 To Washington, this meant extra votes for one government, against the principle of one state, one vote. The question was still unresolved.
How strong a veto can Roosevelt accept? And what is a seat in the Assembly really worth?

## The German question

### Who controls it?
Roosevelt, Churchill and Stalin agreed that Germany must never again be able to start a war. How to prevent it remained unsettled.37,38 In 1944, the European Advisory Commission had prepared American, British and Soviet occupation zones and arrangements for Berlin. Each power would govern its zone after Germany surrendered.39 Whether Germany should also be permanently broken into separate states remained unsettled. Roosevelt and Stalin had supported this idea, but no firm agreement or detailed plan had been reached.39 Churchill could not count on American troops remaining in Germany for long. He therefore pressed for France to share the occupation, with its own zone and a seat on the Allied Control Council. The French zone would be drawn from the British and American zones, leaving the Soviet zone intact.39 However, membership of the governing body would give France a say in decisions affecting Germany as a whole. Stalin had not agreed to that role, and France was not at Yalta to argue for it.2,40
### Who pays?
Controlling Germany also meant deciding what resources it could keep. The Soviet Union had suffered enormous destruction and wanted reparations: German goods, equipment and other resources to compensate for wartime losses.38,40 American advisers supported payments but feared that taking too much would leave the occupying powers paying to support Germany's population.41 Britain shared that concern.42 Removing factories could weaken Germany's ability to wage war, but would also reduce what it could produce to pay reparations later.41 Roosevelt therefore had to consider how demands for compensation would fit with plans to keep Germany disarmed and economically viable.
Should Roosevelt seek agreement on Germany's permanent division while the three leaders were together, or concentrate on the immediate arrangements for occupation? Could a French role help Britain manage an American withdrawal without provoking Soviet opposition? And should reparations be settled now, before the Allies knew how much a defeated Germany could provide?
## Eight days

As the first session opened, Roosevelt knew he could not win everything. Stalin’s armies held Poland. Japan was still fighting. The Senate was watching. His own health was failing.
Every concession on one issue would be paid for on another, and anything left unsettled might be settled without him.
So where should he begin? What could he trade, and with whom? And who, affected by every decision, was not in the room at all?
