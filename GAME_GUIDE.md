# Game guide · Yalta 1945: At the table

Four or five students share one Roosevelt. The facilitator operates the game while the group discusses military cooperation, Poland, the UN and Germany. Allow approximately 30 minutes of play plus debrief time. Game days measure actions, not real elapsed discussion time.

## Aim

Secure agreements you can defend within eight days and the available relationships. Completing every agreement is one possible result, not the only meaningful outcome. Some concessions are costly; leaving a matter unresolved can be deliberate.

Each settled decision has a hidden score from −1 to +2. Explain the scoring rubric before play but keep the individual values hidden. The ending reveals both values and reasons, inviting students to dispute the judgments using evidence about leverage and constraints.

## Facilitation

1. Read the opening briefing and short rules. Explain what the scores assess.
2. Ask the group to justify the issue it opens.
3. Let the group select a proposal. A fictional adviser appears below it in a speech bubble; discuss the two questions while the other proposals remain visible.
4. Confirm **Send proposal** only when the group is ready; **Reconsider** is free.
5. Read the agreement or simulated response. For a counteroffer, discuss the cost of improving terms, accepting a concession or walking away.
6. Watch days and relationships, and consider toasts between decisions.
7. At the ending, compare the revealed scores with the group's own judgments.
8. Reveal the unchanged historical record, then use the supplied debrief questions and print the group summary.

The adviser is an in-game discussion guide, not a named historical official. Its prompts are labelled as dramatisation and do not reveal scores or recommend a correct answer. Clicking another proposal switches the conversation without committing.

Facilitator notes are visible to everyone viewing the screen when F is enabled. Alternative responses and agreements are labelled as teaching simulations. Character dialogue is dramatised rather than a historical quotation.

## What counts as a useful session

Students should be able to explain what they prioritised, what each concession bought, why some demands met resistance, and which interests were left unprotected. A high total is not a substitute for that explanation. The ending displays the total alongside agreements, unresolved decisions, days and relationships.

The most useful final question remains: **Was Yalta a sellout, or the best deal Roosevelt's position allowed?** Keep the group's imagined outcomes distinct from what actually happened.
