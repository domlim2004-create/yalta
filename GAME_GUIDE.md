# Yalta 1945: Game Guide and Intended Outcomes

## What the game is about

**Yalta 1945: The Roosevelt Game** places a group of students in the role of Franklin D. Roosevelt. Together, they must negotiate four issues—Japan, Poland, the United Nations and Germany—while managing eight days and their relationships with Stalin and Churchill.

The intended experience is a discussion about difficult choices. Before selecting an option, players should explain what they hope to gain, whose cooperation they need, and what they are prepared to sacrifice. Every decision affects the time and goodwill available for later negotiations.

The game is designed for 4–5 students sharing one Roosevelt, with a facilitator operating the game on a laptop or projector. Allow about 30 minutes of play, followed by discussion.

## The showcase interface

Open the presidential briefing, then choose among four issue dossiers. During negotiations, the status panel shows days, relationships and decision progress. Proposals lead to sealed telegrams; roll screens show Stalin’s current relationship and the target together. The ending separates completed issues, partly negotiated issues and issues never opened.

The layout prioritises a shared laptop. Facilitator notes are optional and visible to anyone looking at the screen. Long briefings and the historical record can be scrolled. All visuals and text are included in the single offline file.

## What players are working towards

The practical objective is to complete both decisions on all four issues before Roosevelt leaves Yalta. Players must balance three pressures:

- **Time:** Most agreements cost one day. Dead ends and failed rolls consume additional days.
- **Relationships:** Concessions can improve one relationship while damaging another. If either leader becomes hostile, opening negotiations on the UN is blocked.
- **Uncertainty:** Stalin’s cooperation sometimes depends on a die roll. Better relations improve the odds, but cannot guarantee success.

Banquet toasts can repair relationships, but they use time that might otherwise settle an issue.

Each issue’s two decisions must be completed in order before the group chooses another issue. There is one progressing, historical agreement option per decision; the game’s strategy centres on issue order, relationships and time. See [Game terms](GAME_TERMS.md) for an explanation of main lines, dead ends and special cards.

## How to facilitate play

1. Read the opening briefing aloud and let the group discuss which issue to address first.
2. Before each choice, ask players to explain their reasoning. Encourage agreement on a shared decision rather than a quick individual guess.
3. Open the telegram and read its consequences and any character reactions. Reactions are dramatised lines, not historical quotations.
4. When a roll is required, note Stalin’s current score and the target before rolling. Use the on-screen die or enable the physical-die setting.
5. After a dead end or failed roll, discuss what happened. A rejected proposal and an unlucky roll are different kinds of setback.
6. Between decisions, consider whether repairing a relationship is worth spending a day.
7. At the ending, review the group’s outcome before revealing the historical results one row at a time.
8. Finish with the debrief questions. Print the group summary if you want a record of its choices and results.

Press **F** to toggle facilitator notes. The notes provide prompts about leverage, absent parties, enforcement and trade-offs. They are hidden by default.

## What the final outcome should look like

The game ends with a clear record of the group’s conference:

- Completed decisions.
- Unresolved issues and decisions.
- Final relationship scores for Stalin and Churchill.
- Days remaining.

A fully completed game displays:

> You completed every issue before leaving Yalta.

For example, completing **Japan → UN → Germany → Poland**, choosing the agreement options and succeeding on every roll, leaves **one day remaining, Stalin at 5 and Churchill at 2**. This is one possible successful route, not a guaranteed result. Keep this example for facilitator preparation if you want students to discover their own approach.

If the group runs out of days, Roosevelt leaves and unresolved issues remain open. This is a valid ending: it gives the group concrete consequences to discuss.

Zero days prevents starting another decision, even Germany’s second decision, whose agreement option costs zero. If the group has already completed all eight decisions, it receives the completed-game ending.

## Why the historical reveal matters

After reviewing their own outcome, players reveal what Roosevelt obtained historically and what happened afterwards. This introduces the central distinction:

> Reaching an agreement does not guarantee that it will deliver the intended result.

Players may have completed every issue, yet still accepted weak enforcement, concessions affecting absent parties, or decisions deferred to later meetings. A complete board therefore represents success in securing agreements—not proof that every agreement was fair, durable or effective.

The historical reveal compares each issue with the group’s result: **main line reached**, **not reached**, or **never opened**. Use these comparisons to connect the group’s choices with the supplied historical account.

## The desired learning outcome

By the end, students should be able to explain:

- How the order of negotiations affected their later options.
- Which choices spent goodwill they subsequently needed.
- Why some apparently attractive demands failed.
- How concessions affected parties absent from the negotiations.
- How vague wording or deferred decisions helped secure agreement while leaving problems unresolved.
- The difference between a poor decision and an unlucky roll.

The final discussion should move beyond “Did we win?” towards:

> Given Roosevelt’s position, what could we secure, what did it cost, and who would bear the consequences?

The game succeeds when students can defend their choices while recognising their limitations—and discuss whether Yalta was a sellout or the best deal Roosevelt’s position allowed.
